Reflection

A reflection letter addressed to the Curriculum & Instruction Faculty

Dear Faculty of the Curriculum & Instruction Program,

Before the Program. When I began the Master of Education in Curriculum & Instruction at UTRGV, I was in my third year of teaching 8th grade mathematics at Carlos F. Truan Jr. High School in Elsa, Texas. I had recently taken on the roles of PLC Lead and Department Head, and our campus was in the early stages of implementing High-Quality Instructional Materials (HQIM) in partnership with Instruction Partners. At that time, I understood curriculum largely as the materials I was handed and instruction as what I did in front of the classroom. I knew something was missing—our students continued to struggle on STAAR mathematics assessments despite the adoption of research-backed materials—but I lacked the theoretical framework to diagnose why. I entered this program seeking answers to questions I could barely articulate: Why do some curriculum implementations succeed while others stall? What does it truly mean to teach well? And how can assessment become something we do with students rather than to them?

Throughout the Program. The first course that reshaped my thinking was EDCI 7334 (Curriculum Problems and Processes). Studying the four languages of Curricular-Pedagogical Praxis gave me a vocabulary to analyze curriculum decisions that I had been making intuitively. My Design Challenge 2 white paper, "Flipping the Classroom: A Pathway to High-Quality Curriculum Implementation," represents the synthesis of this learning. In it, I analyzed STAAR data from 2013–2024 at my campus, identified the gap between curriculum adoption and implementation fidelity, and proposed the flipped classroom model as a theory-driven intervention.

EDCI 6304 (Assessment of Learning) fundamentally changed how I think about the relationship between assessment and instruction. My Balanced Assessment System project required me to design a coherent system integrating formative assessment for learning, metacognitive assessment as learning, and authentic summative assessment of learning. Collecting qualitative student data through exit tickets and checklists, and quantitative data through rubric-scored performance tasks, taught me that effective assessment is not a single event but a continuous cycle of gathering evidence, interpreting it, and adjusting instruction.

EDFR 6302 (Foundations of Learning, Cognition and Human Development) deepened my understanding of the student as a whole person shaped by cognitive, social, and environmental factors. Learning about behavioral theory, social cognitive theory, and the role of self-efficacy gave me a more nuanced approach to classroom management and student motivation. My module assignment analyzing student learning influences—examining how factors such as socioeconomic background, cultural identity, and prior academic experiences shape readiness to learn—has made me a more empathetic and responsive educator.

EDFR 6300 (Research Methods in Education) equipped me with the tools to engage critically with educational research and to design my own inquiries. Distinguishing between quantitative and qualitative methodologies, evaluating sampling and validity, and formulating researchable questions have become essential in my daily practice as an evidence-informed educator.

My specialization courses—EDCI 7353 (Algebraic Concepts) and EDCI 7354 (Geometric Concepts)—built my pedagogical content knowledge in mathematics. The NCTM Standards essay required me to articulate how the six Principles for School Mathematics apply specifically to algebra instruction, while the Algebra Interview Comparative Report gave me direct experience analyzing how students construct mathematical understanding through clinical interviews.

EDFR 6388 (Historical and Sociocultural Foundations of Education) situated my classroom practice within broader social and historical contexts. Understanding the historical forces that have shaped educational inequality reinforced my commitment to equity-oriented teaching and culturally sustaining pedagogy.

Lessons Learned. If I could give advice to myself at the start of this program, it would be this: teaching is not the delivery of content; it is the design of conditions for learning. Every course in this program converged on the same insight—that the most powerful educators are those who think systematically about curriculum, instruction, assessment, and the whole student, and who use evidence to continuously refine their practice.

I have also learned that meaningful professional growth requires vulnerability. The most valuable learning in this program came not from the times I felt confident but from the moments I was willing to examine my assumptions, acknowledge gaps in my knowledge, and reimagine my practice.

Future Goals and Vision. Looking ahead, I aim to transition from the classroom into curriculum leadership at the campus or district level. My immediate goal is to continue developing as an instructional coach who supports teachers in implementing HQIM with fidelity while adapting instruction to meet the needs of diverse learners. In the longer term, I aspire to contribute to curriculum policy and design at the regional or state level, using the analytical and research skills developed in this program to advocate for equitable mathematics education.

I am particularly committed to exploring how emerging technologies—including artificial intelligence—can be designed and implemented equitably to support rigorous mathematics learning for students from historically underserved communities.

Thank you for the guidance, knowledge, and challenge you have provided throughout this program.

Sincerely,
Abraham Romero Torres

Standard 4 — EDFR 6302

Knowledge of Students

Program completers will demonstrate advanced knowledge of the student as influenced by cognitive, physical, emotional, social, cultural, environmental, and economic factors.

Rationale

EDFR 6302 (Foundations of Learning, Cognition and Human Development) provided a comprehensive framework for understanding the multiple factors that influence students' ability to learn. The course examined cognitive development, social learning theory, motivation, and the role of self-regulation in academic achievement. I learned that students bring unique cognitive profiles, cultural backgrounds, social experiences, and emotional needs to the classroom, and that effective teaching requires attending to all of these dimensions.

For my course project, I created a video presentation on Walter Mischel's theory of delayed gratification — commonly known as the marshmallow test. This theory examines how self-regulation, willpower, and the ability to delay gratification influence cognitive development and academic outcomes. Understanding that students' capacity for self-control is not fixed but can be developed through strategies and environmental supports has fundamentally shaped how I design classroom routines, provide feedback, and build student agency in my mathematics classroom.

These artifacts demonstrate my ability to connect major learning theories to concrete instructional practice — recognizing that factors like self-regulation, motivation, cultural background, and systemic inequality directly impact students' ability to learn.

Standard 6 — EDCI 6304

Knowledge of Assessment

Program completers will demonstrate advanced knowledge of assessment and the ability to use multiple sources of assessment for maximizing student learning.

Rationale

EDCI 6304 (Assessment of Learning) provided a comprehensive framework for understanding assessment as an integrated system serving three purposes: assessment for learning (formative), assessment as learning (metacognitive), and assessment of learning (summative). The course emphasized that effective assessment is not a single event but a complete six-stage cycle in which each component informs the others and collectively drives instructional decisions.

The artifacts below document the complete assessment cycle I designed for an 8th-grade mathematics unit on calculating the volume of cylinders, aligned to Category 3 of the 8th-grade mathematics Texas Essential Knowledge and Skills (TEKS).

Stage 1: Setting Objectives for Learning

This artifact highlights the design and implementation of an assessment cycle that begins with the establishment of objectives for all three types:

  • Assessment for Learning (formative): The learner will calculate volume of cylinders using the volume formula based on given dimensions throughout Module 5 Topic 4 Lesson 1.
  • Assessment as Learning (metacognitive): The learner will explain the process that the volume formula uses to calculate the capacity of a cylinder by describing the relationship between radius, height, and volume on an Exit Ticket.
  • Assessment of Learning (summative): The learner will analyze the volume of rainfall during a major hurricane and design a rainfall harvesting system based on their analysis.

Stage 2: Planning Summative Assessment — Rainwater Harvesting PBL

This artifact documents the design and implementation of a Project-Based Learning (PBL) authentic assessment focused on designing a rainwater harvesting system. Key features include:

  • Students calculate potential rainwater volume based on storm data, catchment area, and surface material.
  • Emphasis on precise volume calculations: Students meticulously calculate the volume of cylindrical storage tanks (sourced from National Storage Tank) using the formula for the volume of a cylinder.
  • Direct comparison of calculated rainwater volume and tank capacity ensures appropriate tank selection.
  • Students design a non-scaled model of their system.
  • A final report details calculations, design choices, and model components.
  • Assessment rubric focuses on accuracy of calculations, correct formula application, and conceptual understanding.

Note: This assessment serves as both the revised assessment and the implemented assessment.

Standard 8 — Any course

Integrating Technology with Instruction

Program completers will demonstrate ability to integrate current technology into instruction and communications/collaboration activities where appropriate.

Rationale

EDCI 6348 provided the opportunity to develop a concept paper proposing the Matrix Classroom — an AI-powered executable lesson platform that represents the most significant technology integration project of my graduate career. The platform uses artificial intelligence not as a student-facing tutor but as a teacher-facing amplifier: a system that automates the repetitive, timing-sensitive aspects of lesson delivery while giving the teacher real-time situational awareness of every student's understanding. Below I address the four dimensions of this standard using the Matrix Classroom as the primary artifact, supplemented by a second technology example from a previous course.

Types of technology used: The Matrix Classroom integrates multiple technology layers: the Matrix protocol (self-hosted Synapse homeserver with E2EE) as the encrypted communication and delivery medium; generative AI orchestration (Hermes classroom-bot profile) as the lesson facilitator running the campus 5-step instructional cycle; Honcho vector memory (pgvector + Redis) for per-student longitudinal tracking across 51 lessons; computer vision (snap-photo math input) for eliminating the typing barrier in middle school mathematics; and Cloudflare Tunnel for secure public access behind a self-hosted firewall.

Developing conceptual understanding and instruction: The platform operationalizes the Bluebonnet Learning (TEA OER) pedagogical model as a state-machine curriculum — each lesson is a runnable program with typed slots for Bell Ringer, Hook, Direct Instruction blocks, 3A Talk discourse prompts, Practice problems, and Exit Tickets. The AI bot posts timed prompts, collects student responses, and routes them to Honcho memory, enabling real-time misconception detection and forgetting-curve spiral review. This is not a common presentation tool; it is an entirely new instructional delivery medium that transforms how curriculum is executed in the classroom.

Communication and collaboration: Matrix encrypted chat rooms serve as the communication medium for all instructional discourse. Students engage in structured 3A Talk using sentence stems, collaborate in team rooms during Learning Together phases, and receive personalized warm-up problems via DM at check-in. A Teacher Radar system provides the teacher a private DM channel with real-time alerts about stuck teams, misconception clusters, and silent students — converting the noise of 21 typing students into actionable instructional intelligence.

Fostering collaboration: The platform enables dynamic breakout grouping — students are auto-grouped into reteach and extension sub-rooms based on real-time Honcho assessment data. The Lesson Playback Studio captures every class session as a replayable artifact with misconception heatmaps, enabling absent-student replay and auto-generated small-group plans for the next day. This technology infrastructure is already deployed and running on self-hosted infrastructure, demonstrating the ability to integrate current technology into instruction in a pedagogically meaningful way.
